13.0 Losses, Retreats, Rout, and Surrender

A defeated division is not automatically eliminated. It first tries to retreat. If it cannot retreat, or if its morale and cohesion collapse, it may rout or surrender.

This chapter describes the less glorious but more important part of the system: what happens to a division after a defeat. A repulsed attack or a lost trench combat does not simply make units vanish; it triggers a chain of decisions and checks — retreat, where, at what cost, or hold in place at the risk of surrender — which chapter 12.0 deliberately leaves to this section so as not to overload the resolution of combat itself. It is useful to read this chapter together with 12.0: every combat result refers back here for its long-term operational consequences.

13.1 Voluntary Retreat

A voluntary retreat must be written in the orders. It is used to save a division before it is worn down, encircled, or destroyed.

Retreating by choice, before the situation collapses, is often the hardest decision for a player to make: it means giving up ground the enemy has not yet taken by force. The system still rewards this prudent choice by making it cheap (0 or 1 LP) compared to the far higher cost, in Morale and Fatigue, of a forced retreat suffered after a defeat. A shrewd commander will use a voluntary retreat to save an already tired division before it is exposed to an attack it could hardly withstand.

Type Cost Conditions Effect
Fall back one line within the same sector 0 LP Division not isolated, not contested, valid route. The division falls back one line.
Retreat into an adjacent friendly sector 1 LP Adjacent friendly sector, capacity available, valid route. The division enters the friendly sector, normally in Line 3.
  • A division that retreats voluntarily cannot attack the same turn.
  • If it retreats from Line 1 or from a bombarded sector the same turn, it gains +1 Fatigue.
  • If the enemy attacks the same line, Operational Initiative decides whether the retreat happens first.

13.2 Forced Retreat

A forced retreat is imposed by a combat, morale, or rout result.

Unlike a voluntary retreat, this is never a choice: it is the direct consequence of an event that has already happened on the field. A division does not decide to retreat forcibly; it suffers the retreat as an automatic effect of one of the results listed below, and pays a higher price in Fatigue and Morale than a voluntary retreat, as shown in table 13.4.

A division must retreat forcibly if:

  • it loses a trench combat by 3 or more successes;
  • it suffers a local breakthrough;
  • it seriously fails a morale test;
  • it is at Morale 1 and would lose further Morale;
  • it is at Fatigue 5 and loses a combat.

13.3 Retreat Routes

A retreating division, whether voluntarily or forcibly, always follows the same pattern: it first tries to fall back within its own sector, then toward an adjacent friendly sector, and only as a last resort risks a rout. This hierarchy reflects the defensive logic of the three lines from chapter 4.0: Line 3 also exists to give a repulsed division somewhere to reorganize without having to immediately abandon the sector.

Current position First choice Second choice Third choice
Line 1 Line 2, same sector Line 3, same sector Adjacent friendly sector
Line 2 Line 3, same sector Adjacent friendly sector Rout
Line 3 Adjacent friendly sector Sector toward the rear Rout
Contested line Free friendly line in the same sector Adjacent friendly sector Rout or surrender

A route is valid if it is not occupied exclusively by the enemy, does not exceed capacity, does not cross an enemy sector, and, if possible, moves closer to the rear.

13.4 Effects of Retreat

The table below sums up why it is almost always better to retreat voluntarily rather than wait for a forced retreat: the latter always involves a loss of Morale as well as Fatigue, and aggravating circumstances (bombarded sector, difficult route) stack instead of replacing one another.

Type Effect
Ordered voluntary retreat +1 Fatigue if from Line 1 or under bombardment.
Forced retreat +1 Fatigue and -1 Morale.
Through a bombarded or interdicted sector An additional +1 Fatigue.
Difficult but possible retreat Loses 1,000 men, then retreats.
Impossible retreat Take a surrender test.

13.5 Rout

A rout represents a chaotic retreat and the temporary loss of operational control.

A routed division is no longer a reliable tool in the player's hands: for several turns it cannot be used offensively or reconstituted, and it will still take up space and capacity in the rear. It is the state in the system closest to what really happened when a unit lost cohesion under the pressure of fire: the problem was no longer winning the fight, but simply bringing the men back under functioning command.

  • The division falls back toward the nearest rear area.
  • It loses 1,000 men.
  • It gains +2 Fatigue.
  • It loses 1 Morale, minimum 1.
  • It cannot attack.
  • It cannot receive offensive orders until it recovers.
  • It cannot receive replacements while routed.

13.6 When to Test for Surrender

The surrender test is the last resort: it applies only when retreating is no longer an available option, or when isolation and attrition have already pushed a division to the limits described in chapter 5.0. Precisely listing the cases that trigger it, as the list below does, prevents the test from being called for arbitrarily: if none of these conditions is met, the division does not test for surrender, no matter how desperate its situation may look.

A division must take a surrender test if:

  • it must retreat but has no valid route;
  • it is isolated and at Morale 1;
  • it is isolated and at Fatigue 5;
  • it is on a contested line, loses the combat, and cannot fall back;
  • it has fewer than 3,000 men and suffers a forced retreat;
  • it is hit by gas, loses Morale, and has no way out;
  • it is in a surrounded fort and fails the morale test.

13.7 Surrender Test

The surrender test formula deliberately mirrors that of the morale test in chapter 5.6, but subtracts Fatigue instead of ignoring it: at this critical moment, physical wear counts as much as the will to fight. The modifiers in the table below reflect the same circumstances already seen elsewhere in the manual — Experience, isolation, fortification, gas, losses — all applied together at the moment they matter most.

13.8 Effects of Surrender

Surrender, unlike a rout, is a permanent state: it is not a division that will reorganize in later turns, but a permanent loss for the current scenario. The consequences listed below therefore apply for the rest of the game, not just for the turn in which the surrender happens.

  • A division removed by surrender cannot receive replacements for the rest of the scenario.
  • MGs on the line are captured or eliminated.
  • The line passes to the winner if no enemy units remain.
  • The scenario may award VP for surrendered divisions.
  • A surrender in a fort may award additional VP.
Example: F02 is isolated, at Morale 1 and Fatigue 5. It must retreat but has no valid route. It rolls for surrender: 1d12 +1 -5 -2 isolation -2 no route. Even with a good roll, it risks a rout or full surrender.
