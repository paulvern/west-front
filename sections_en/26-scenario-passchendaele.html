25.0 Scenario: Western Front — Passchendaele 1917

Not an enemy to defeat, but ground to cross: here the real opponent for both players is the mud, and neither side can choose not to fight it.

25.1 Introduction to the Scenario

This scenario represents the Second Battle of Passchendaele (26 October - 10 November 1917), the culminating and final phase of the Third Battle of Ypres. The Canadian Corps, just arrived to relieve Australian, New Zealand, and British divisions already at the end of their strength, must capture the ridge and village of Passchendaele before winter. The German player must defend a line of concrete strongpoints scattered through the mud, knowing that every metre lost will cost both sides dearly.

It had rained without stopping since 19 October, even before the battle began. The commander of the Canadian Corps, General Arthur Currie, predicted the operation would cost around 16,000 Canadian casualties — an estimate that would prove almost exact — and objected to the order, but was overruled. Victory conditions are gathered separately, in chapter 26.0.

Historical note: one fact complicates the scenario's reputation as pure one-sided slaughter: in the window from 21 October to 10 November, German losses are estimated at around 30,000, nearly double the 15,634 Canadian losses. The mud slowed and wore down both sides, but Allied artillery — nearly 2,000 guns deployed — kept hitting hard even through the rain.

25.2 Duration

The scenario lasts 6 turns, corresponding to the four real phases of the Canadian attack: 26 October (Turns 1-2), 30 October (Turn 3), 6 November — the capture of the village (Turns 4-5) — and the final action of 10 November (Turn 6), which straightened the line without ever securing the northern end of the ridge.

25.6 The Mud of Passchendaele

This is the rule that defines the scenario, and unlike the mines at Messines or the flooding of the Piave it is not a single event: it is a condition that lasts from the first turn to the last, exactly as the real rain lasted for weeks.

25.6.2 Additional Effects of Rain/Mud

In addition to the effects already covered by the base rulebook (chapter 11.0, air missions), in this scenario a turn of Rain/Mud also means:

  • Every internal or operational movement costs 1 additional LP over the normal amount.
  • If an assault gets a result of "attack repulsed" or "mutual losses" (12.4), the attacking division gains +1 additional Fatigue beyond what is already called for: falling back through the mud under enemy fire costs more than falling back on dry ground.
Why it works this way: at Passchendaele men advanced through mud up to their waists, often unable to free their feet from the mire. Artillery pieces sank, wooden duckboard tracks were the only way to move supplies, and shell craters filled with rainwater became deadly traps just as much as enemy fire.

25.6.3 Duckboard Tracks

Action Cost Feasible if... Effect
Build a duckboard track 1 LP Controlled sector, not contested, not bombarded the same turn. Movement through that sector no longer suffers the additional mud cost (section 25.6.2), until the track is lost.

A duckboard track is automatically considered lost if the sector becomes contested or passes under enemy control, and must be rebuilt from scratch if the sector returns to friendly control.

25.7 Concrete Strongpoints

Unlike the continuous trench lines seen in the other scenarios, the German defense in Flanders relied on isolated reinforced-concrete strongpoints, able to support each other with crossing fire and far more resistant to bombardment than ordinary earth trenches.

Rule: in sectors P1-P8, artillery must obtain 2 successes instead of 1 to reduce a line's fortification by one level. The concrete absorbs bombardment that would have levelled an earth trench in a single strike.

25.8 Limited Objectives

Currie, like Plumer before him at Messines (chapter 19.9), refused to launch a single ambitious assault: instead he planned four separate, limited blows, each carefully prepared before being delivered, rather than chasing all the ground at once.

  • If the British declares, in the same turn, assaults against more than two different P1-P8 sectors, every division involved in a third or later assault that turn suffers VT worsened by 1: artillery preparation cannot adequately cover more than two objectives at once at this level of logistics.
  • If the British respects this limit for the entire scenario, he receives the historical success bonus described in section 26.1.

25.12 The Context of the Battle

The Third Battle of Ypres had begun on 31 July 1917 with the goal of pushing the Germans away from the Belgian Channel ports, a base for the submarines threatening Allied supply routes. Month after month, the advance had bogged down in a swamp of water-filled craters and German defenses organized in depth, with concrete strongpoints instead of continuous trenches. By mid-October, with British, Australian, and New Zealand divisions now at the end of their strength, Haig turned to the Canadian Corps to finish capturing the ridge before winter.

Currie objected openly, calling the operation a needless risk, but once he received the order he devoted two weeks to the most meticulous preparation possible: roads, light railways, artillery positions moved forward one gun at a time, under conditions that would have discouraged almost anyone else.

Operational reading: Passchendaele works, in the game as in reality, as a test of logistics even before a test of combat: every LP spent on a duckboard track or on movement through the mud is time and resources taken away from the actual assault. This is not a scenario won by attacking more: it is won by attacking better, with fewer wasted moves.

25.13 Bellevue and the Assault of 26 October

Historical context: the first Canadian assault, on 26 October, involved the 3rd and 4th Divisions. It had been raining for a week; the attack bogged down almost immediately, and the 4th Division was pushed back to within a few dozen metres of its starting point. It was the hardest day of the entire operation.

Sectors P1 Wallemolen and P2 Bellevue represent this first, brutal clash.

25.14 Crest Farm and Meetcheele, 30 October

Historical context: the second assault, on 30 October, brought minimal gains at a terrible price — the 78th Canadian Brigade lost half its strength in a single day. Canadian patrols did reach the village, however, finding the Germans already withdrawing: a sign the defense was about to give way, even though it would take another week to confirm it.

Sectors P4 Crest Farm and P5 Meetcheele represent this second phase, the one in which the village first seemed within reach.

25.15 The Village, 6 November

Historical context: the 1st and 2nd Canadian Divisions, held in reserve for weeks, captured the ruins of Passchendaele on 6 November. Nine Victoria Crosses were awarded to Canadian soldiers for actions in this battle, including James Peter Robertson, killed in this final assault.

Sector P6 Passchendaele represents the objective that gives the battle, and the entire campaign, its name: a heap of rubble, no longer a village in any real sense, but a symbol that was worth, in the eyes of command, every single loss needed to take it.

25.16 Westrozebeke, Beyond the Ridge

Historical context: the final action of 10 November straightened the line north of the village, but the Germans kept the northern end of the ridge. The British advance stopped just short of Westrozebeke: an objective never reached, just like Cambrai for the offensive three weeks later, or Treviso for the Isonzo.

Sector P8 Westrozebeke represents this limit never reached. Going beyond it, in the scenario, means achieving an operational success that never happened in reality.
