Part I — Base Rules

Rules common to all scenarios.

1.0 General Principles

The game is designed to be played with paper and pen, on sheets printed at home, using very simple maps that schematically represent the battlefield — but the maps are not used to place counters or physical units. Each sheet is a plain text file, editable and printable to suit your needs. 
Each scenario can require many sheets and generate a bit of clutter. The game is not designed to be fast, nor to help the player avoid mistakes. The basic idea is to represent the general staff of two armies during the planning phases of major battles of the First World War. Generals were almost never at the front line, information was almost never certain, and mistakes were an everyday occurrence. The game uses a system based on Logistics Points (LP from here on) in which each general staff has a certain number of actions available covering any part of the battle, from planning bombardments to giving direct orders to divisions. LP represent the availability of munitions, the use of friendly lines to move, withdraw, and reinforce divisions, and the use of aviation, artillery, and gas. Deliberately, ordering an attack costs no LP at all. In the First World War, an attack order consisted of an officer's whistle: the troops poured out of the trenches, and there was not much left to do but watch how it turned out.

Every action in the game follows five questions:

  1. When is it declared? In which phase it is written down (everything is written on paper!) or chosen.
  2. How much does it cost? In LP, or 0 LP if free.
  3. Is it feasible? Which conditions must be met.
  4. How is it resolved? A die roll, a procedure, or an automatic application.
  5. What is the effect? What changes on the map, on the units, or on resources.

This five-question structure is not a stylistic flourish: it is the tool that lets two players resolve any disagreement about the rules without having to guess the author's intent. If a doubt about an action comes up during a game — "can I do this now?", "do I have to pay for it?", "which die do I resolve it with?" — the answer is always found by working through these five questions in the order they are asked. First the timing is established (the correct phase), then the cost, then feasibility, then the resolution procedure, and finally the concrete effect. Chapter 2.0 systematically applies this scheme to every action in the game in table form, while chapter 3.0 shows how the phases follow one another over the course of a full turn.

Golden rule: if an action has an LP cost and it is not paid during the Logistics Phase, the action cannot be carried out. If it requires an order and it is not written during the Orders Phase, the action cannot be carried out.

The golden rule exists to stop a player from "finding" resources or intentions once combat has already begun. Logistics Points must be committed before knowing for certain how the opponent will react, exactly as a 1916 commander had to route munitions and orders toward a sector well before knowing the outcome of the engagement. In the same way, an order written late — after simultaneous revelation — has no effect at all: the simultaneity of orders, described in full in chapter 7.0, is what gives the system its characteristic tension of command games, in which every plan must reckon with uncertainty about what the opponent is doing at that very moment.

1.1 Costs of 0 LP

Some actions cost 0 LP, but they are not "free" in an absolute sense. They may require a phase, an order, a condition, or be the mandatory consequence of a result.

In other words, the LP cost measures only one of the game's resources: the logistical one. A 0 LP action can still "cost" time (it must happen in the right phase), initiative (it must be written as an order and therefore revealed to the opponent), or operational conditions (a suppressed machine gun cannot fire even though defensive fire is free).
