23.0 Scenario: Western Front — Cambrai 1917

The first scenario in this manual with two genuine plot twists: the tank-driven surprise that tears open the Hindenburg Line in a matter of hours, and the German counterattack that, ten days later, takes back almost everything that was gained.

23.1 Introduction to the Scenario

This scenario represents the Battle of Cambrai (20 November - 7 December 1917): the first massed tank assault in history, followed by the most effective German counterattack on the Western Front up to that point. The British player must exploit tactical surprise and the power of tanks to break through the Hindenburg Line and push toward Cambrai itself, an objective never actually reached in reality. The German player must absorb the initial shock, then turn the game around with the new infiltration tactics that Crown Prince Rupprecht of Bavaria would launch on 30 November.

Unlike Messines and the Piave — where one player starts with an overwhelming advantage and keeps it for the whole scenario — Cambrai is built on two opposing phases. In the first, both the initiative and the mechanical advantage belong entirely to the British. In the second, the initiative passes to the Germans, who respond not with more men but with a different way of using them. Victory conditions are gathered separately, in chapter 24.0.

Historical note: of the 476 Mark IV tanks deployed on 20 November, by the end of the first day 65 had been destroyed, 71 had broken down, and 43 were ditched — almost 38% out of action within hours, before even counting the losses of the following days. No scenario in this manual until now has had to represent a weapon that was simultaneously this decisive and this unreliable.

23.2 Duration

The scenario lasts 8 turns. Turns 1-4 represent the British assault and the consolidation fighting around Bourlon Wood in the following days; from Turn 5, the initiative passes to the German counterattack of 30 November, which in reality recovered most of the lost ground within a few days.

23.7 Tank Battalions

Tank battalions are a support asset available only to the British in this scenario, distinguished from MG and artillery by three properties.

23.7.1 Direct Breakthrough

Rule: a tank battalion assigned to an assault cancels, for that assault only, both the penalty for attacking without reconnaissance or preparatory bombardment (chap. 12.2) and the target line's defensive fortification bonus. The way no longer needs to be cleared by days of bombardment: the tank clears it on the spot, on the spot.

23.7.2 Mechanical Unreliability

Procedure, before every combat involving tanks:
  1. For every tank battalion assigned to the assault, roll 1d12.
  2. On 1-4, the battalion has broken down for this turn: it does not roll dice, does not cancel any penalty or defensive bonus, and simply does not take part.
  3. On 5+, the battalion is operational and section 23.7.1 applies normally.
  4. A broken-down battalion may become operational again in later turns: roll again every time it is assigned to a new assault.

23.7.3 Vulnerability to Anti-Tank Fire

Procedure:
  1. If the German has at least one artillery battery not already committed to another mission that turn, he may declare direct anti-tank fire against a single operational tank battalion involved in the assault.
  2. The battery rolls 2d12; every result of 9+ is a success.
  3. With at least 1 success, the tank battalion is destroyed: permanently removed from the scenario, not merely suppressed.
  4. A battery that declares anti-tank fire cannot also use that mission to bombard the line the same turn.
Historical example: at Flesquières, tank D51 "Deborah" was struck by five direct hits from a single German field gun and destroyed on the spot. This is exactly the scene that section 23.7.3 translates into a rule.

Destroyed tank battalions cannot be replaced during the scenario: production could not keep pace within a battle lasting only a few weeks.

23.8 Total Surprise, Turn 1

Procedure, Turn 1 only:
  1. No German division in sectors C1, C2, C3, and C4 may use defensive MG fire in the first assault it suffers this turn: the machine guns cannot be manned in time.
  2. Every German division in those sectors gains +1 Fatigue from the total surprise.
  3. The British automatically has Operational Initiative in Turn 1, regardless of the roll.

Unlike the mines at Messines, the surprise here does not come from a single physical event that destroys the defenses, but from the deliberate absence of a preparatory bombardment: the Germans simply did not expect the attack, and so were not ready to receive it.

23.9 Anti-Tank Fire as an Action

Cross-reference to section 23.7.3: anti-tank fire is the only defensive action in this manual able to remove a support asset permanently, rather than suppressing it for a turn. It represents a real choice for the German — use a battery to bombard a line, or hold it in reserve to strike a tank if and when one appears.

23.10 The Bridge at Masnières

Procedure: if a British division successfully crosses into C7 Masnières thanks to an operational tank battalion, roll 1d12: on 1-6 the canal bridge collapses under the tank's weight, and no further British division may use the C7-C8 connection for the rest of the scenario; on 7+ the bridge holds and the connection remains open normally.
Why it works this way: at Masnières, the canal bridge really did collapse under a tank's weight, preventing the entire 2nd Cavalry Division from crossing and exploiting the breakthrough on the right flank. It was one of the reasons a huge tactical success never translated into an operational victory: the road to exploit it was, quite literally, missing.

23.11 The German Counterattack

Procedure, from Turn 5 onward:
  1. The German receives, one time only in Turn 5, +7 special LP for the counterattack.
  2. From Turn 5 onward, the first German division to attack each turn receives VT improved by 1, for the new infiltration tactics.
  3. The German reserves listed in section 23.13.2 already represent this influx: no additional reinforcements stack beyond those listed there.

Crown Prince Rupprecht of Bavaria's tactics did not rely on more men, but on a different method: a short bombardment instead of a prolonged one, storm units that bypass pockets of resistance instead of attacking them head-on, immediate exploitation of every gap. It is the same principle, applied in defense rather than attack, that would become central to the German spring offensive of 1918.

23.12 No British Preparatory Bombardment

In Turn 1, the British may not declare a preparatory bombardment before the assault: any prolonged bombardment would have destroyed the surprise that makes section 23.8 possible. From Turn 2 onward, preparatory bombardment becomes available normally.

23.16 The Context of the Battle of Cambrai

After the grinding, tragic Passchendaele offensive, concluded only weeks earlier, British command was looking for a success that would restore confidence among an Allied public also unsettled by the gradual unravelling of the Russian front. Cambrai, a key rail hub behind the Hindenburg Line, was chosen not for its immediate strategic value, but because the area's dry, chalky ground was ideal for tanks — the exact opposite of Passchendaele's mud.

The plan, worked out largely by General Julian Byng and General Hugh Elles' Tank Corps, turned the logic of every previous offensive on its head: no prolonged bombardment that would announce the attack days in advance, but artillery "silently registered" beforehand and unleashed only at zero hour, together with hundreds of tanks that would clear the way through the barbed wire and trenches.

Operational reading: Cambrai works, in the game as in reality, as two scenarios in one. In the first half, the British must exploit an enormous but fleeting advantage before tank unreliability and a shortage of reserves consume it on their own. In the second half, the German must prove that a better method can be worth as much as — or more than — material advantage.

23.17 Flesquières and the Gun That Stopped a Tank

Historical context: at Flesquières, the only point on the front where the British attack was seriously slowed on the first day, a German artillery officer stayed at his field gun and single-handedly destroyed several British tanks, including the famous D51 "Deborah," hit by five direct rounds. The commander of the 51st Highland Division, Harper, had also chosen not to advance infantry and tanks in close coordination, further complicating the assault in this sector.

Sector C3 Flesquières represents this episode: the one point on the map where the German may use anti-tank fire even on a contested line, as an exception to the general rule (section 23.6).

23.18 Bourlon Wood, the Objective Never Truly Secured

Historical context: the wood and ridge of Bourlon dominated the entire sector from the north and were the most ambitious objective in the British plan. They were only partly captured, after days of brutal fighting, and were never secured before the German counterattack began: a perfect symbol of a tactical success that never managed to become a stable operational advantage.

Sector C5 Bourlon Wood represents this never-truly-secured objective: the wooded terrain penalizes an attack without reconnaissance just as much as it favors the defense.

23.19 Cambrai, the City Never Reached

Historical context: despite the deepest single-day breakthrough on the Western Front since the end of mobile warfare in 1914, British troops never got close enough to see Cambrai. The German counterattack of 30 November, led by Crown Prince Rupprecht of Bavaria, recovered most of the lost ground within a few days: in the end, each side had suffered around 40,000 casualties, for a territorial balance close to zero.

Sector C8 Cambrai represents this limit never reached. Like Treviso for the Isonzo, or Verdun itself for France, it is the objective that gives the battle its name precisely because it was never captured.
