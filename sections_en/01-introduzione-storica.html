Historical Introduction

The Western Front as a war of attrition, logistics, and operational decision-making.

The Western Front of the First World War was not a static war, but one in which every movement came at enormous cost. Trenches, artillery, machine guns, mud, wire entanglements, forts, and broken communications turned every advance into a complex operational problem.

After 1914, the war of movement gave way to a deep war of position. Capturing a few kilometers took more than simply "attacking": it required massing artillery, observing the target, cutting or crossing obstacles, suppressing machine guns, bringing up reserves, and keeping enough cohesion to withstand counterattacks.

This hardening of the front was not an accident, but the logical consequence of a series of technological and doctrinal imbalances. The machine gun multiplied the defensive firepower of a few dozen well-placed men; barbed wire slowed the attacker at the very moment he was most exposed; artillery, while capable of destroying a position, rarely destroyed it thoroughly enough to prevent it from being defended again in the minutes after the bombardment lifted. The result was what the Germans called Materialschlacht, the "battle of materiel": a contest in which local tactical victory mattered less than the ability to feed the front with men, shells, and supplies longer than the opponent could. It is exactly this logic of attrition and resource management that the game system aims to put in the player's hands.

The game system represents this reality in operational terms. The player does not control individual platoons, but decides where to spend resources, where to strike, when to move reserves, when to retreat, and when to risk an assault.

Concretely, this means the player does not "win" a battle simply by ordering an attack: he must first build the conditions that make that attack worthwhile. He must decide whether it is worth spending Logistics Points on a preparatory bombardment, whether to request aerial observation to make artillery more accurate, whether to wait for fresh reserves to arrive, or whether to accept the risk of attacking with divisions already worn down by fatigue. Every choice has an opportunity cost: LP spent on gas are no longer available for counter-battery fire, and divisions committed to one sector cannot cover another. The manual that follows translates this logic into precise procedures, so that every decision at the table reflects a real trade-off that a 1916 commander would have had to face.

Verdun, February 1916: the German offensive against Verdun began on 21 February 1916 with a bombardment of enormous intensity. In the first days, the German operational objective was to overrun the forward French positions on the right bank of the Meuse, opening the way to key forts, heights, and villages.
The role of the forts: Douaumont, Vaux, and Souville were not merely military works. They were points of moral and political reference. Their fall or their resistance had effects far beyond their pure tactical value. For this reason, in the scenario, the forts affect both operational control and Victory Points.

The rest of this manual is organized into four parts. Part I (chapters 1.0–14.0) contains the base ruleset, valid for any scenario played with this system: general principles, the catalog of actions, the turn sequence, sectors and lines, divisions, logistics, orders, movement, artillery, gas, aviation, combat, retreats, and fortifications. Part II (chapters 15.0–24.0) applies these rules to the introductory scenario, the Gateway to Verdun 1916, with its own map, forces, logistics, and victory conditions. Part III collects the printable sheets needed to play without having to copy tables out by hand, and Part IV offers a series of complete examples that walk step by step through the application of the trickier rules. Readers encountering this manual for the first time may choose to proceed in order, chapter by chapter, or to read Part II first to get a concrete sense of the game and then come back to clarify the details in Part I.
