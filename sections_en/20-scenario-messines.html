19.0 Scenario: Western Front — Messines 1917

Nineteen mines, one ridge, one week: the shortest and most lopsided scenario in this manual, where for once it is the defender who has the real problem.

19.1 Introduction to the Scenario

This scenario represents the Battle of Messines (7-14 June 1917), the operation by which the British Second Army under General Sir Herbert Plumer captured the Messines-Wytschaete ridge south of Ypres, an indispensable preliminary to the much larger and far more tragic Passchendaele offensive that would follow a few weeks later. The British player must exploit the opening advantage — nineteen mines detonated simultaneously beneath the German lines — to capture the entire ridge within the historical timeframe, without pushing beyond the planned final objective. The German player must absorb a shock that no amount of preparation could truly cushion, and then try to limit the damage with what little is left.

Unlike Verdun and the Isonzo, the starting imbalance here is not a side effect of the historical situation: it is the point of the scenario. By the standards of the Great War, Messines was an almost "safe" operation: eighteen months of tunnelling work, over 450 tonnes of explosive, 2,000 guns, infantry training rehearsed meticulously on scale replicas of the terrain in the rear. General Plumer reportedly told his officers on the eve of the attack: "We may not make history tomorrow, but we shall certainly change the geography." Victory conditions are gathered separately, in chapter 20.0.

Historical note: of the twenty-two mines dug, nineteen exploded at 3:10 am on 7 June 1917. One, at Petite Douve Farm, had been discovered and destroyed by German counter-miners in August 1916; two, near Ploegsteert Wood, fell outside the attack area and were never fired. One of these last two went off on its own in 1955, struck by lightning during a thunderstorm: the only casualty was a cow. The other has never been found.

19.2 Duration

The scenario lasts 6 turns, the shortest in this manual. This is not a convenience: it reflects the fact that the battle's outcome was largely decided in the first hours of 7 June, with the entire ridge in British hands by mid-morning. The remaining turns represent the consolidation and the German counterattacks against the Oosttaverne Line, the final objective reached on 14 June — not a new battle, but the management of a success already achieved.

19.7 The Mines of Messines

This is the special rule that defines the scenario. It is resolved once, before the Orders Phase of Turn 1, and never again.

Procedure, Turn 1 only:
  1. For each of sectors M1, M2, M3, M5, M6 (Mine terrain), identify Line 1.
  2. Every German division present on that line immediately gains +2 Fatigue and takes a morale test at -2 to the roll.
  3. Every German MG present on that line is suppressed for all of Turn 1.
  4. If the British player declares an assault against that line in Turn 1, the assault receives 3 additional automatic successes, before any dice are rolled.
  5. These effects apply to no other line, no other sector, and no later turn.
Why it works this way: nineteen mines, nearly 450 tonnes of explosive, blew the entire German front line apart in a matter of seconds. The survivors, dazed, offered almost no resistance in many places: the Second Army took over 7,000 prisoners that morning alone. This is not an advantage that can be "played better" or repeated: it is a fixed, enormous, one-time event, exactly as it was in reality.

19.8 Preparatory Bombardment

In Turn 1, the British player may resolve two preparatory bombardment missions instead of just one (paying normally for each), to represent the 2,000 guns deployed and the weeks of preparatory fire that preceded the assault. From Turn 2 onward the normal limit of one mission per battery per turn applies.

19.9 "Bite and Hold" Doctrine

Plumer planned Messines with deliberately limited objectives — the ridge and the Oosttaverne Line, not a metre more — precisely to avoid creating an exposed salient that the Germans could cut off with a counterattack, as had already happened elsewhere on the front. This rule translates that caution into a mechanical incentive.

  • If at the end of a turn the British operationally controls a sector beyond M8 Oosttaverne Line (none exists on the base map: this rule only matters if a variant of the scenario adds deeper sectors), his divisions in that sector gain +1 Fatigue for having pushed beyond the planned supply line.
  • If the British reaches operational control of every planned objective (M1-M8) without ever going beyond them, he receives the historical success bonus described in section 20.1.

19.10 Delayed German Reinforcements

German lines of communication in the Messines-Wytschaete pocket were largely cut or rendered useless by the bombardment and the shock of the mines. German reserves enter one turn later than the availability stated in section 19.13 if the entry sector is adjacent to a sector the British operationally controls at that moment.

19.14 The Context of the Battle of Messines

After the failure of the Nivelle Offensive on the Chemin des Dames in spring 1917 and the mutinies that followed in the French army, the weight of the Allied offensive effort on the Western Front fell largely to the British. British command wanted to launch a major offensive in Flanders to clear the Belgian coast of German U-boat bases; but first the Messines-Wytschaete salient had to be eliminated, since since 1914 it had given the Germans excellent observation over the entire Ypres salient.

General Plumer, commanding the Second Army, planned the operation with a methodical care that earned him a reputation as the least reckless and most reliable commander in the British Army. The infantry trained for weeks on scale replicas of the actual terrain; the artillery was coordinated down to the smallest detail; and beneath the ridge, for over a year, companies of British, Australian, New Zealand, and Canadian tunnellers dug more than 8,000 metres of galleries, often just metres from their German counterparts engaged in the same underground war.

Operational reading: Messines works, in the game as in reality, because each phase is set up by the one before: the mines open the breach, the bombardment isolates the reserves, the infantry occupies ground already largely emptied of defenders able to resist. It is the opposite of the logic of Verdun, where every turn of combat has to earn its own success.

19.15 The Mines and the Ridge

Historical context: at 3:10 am on 7 June 1917, nineteen mines exploded almost simultaneously along the ridge. According to various contemporary accounts, the explosion was heard as far away as London. General Plumer reportedly told his officers on the eve of the attack: "We may not make history tomorrow, but we shall certainly change the geography." Within a few hours, the entire ridge — Hill 60, St. Eloi, Wytschaete, Spanbroekmolen, Messines — was in British hands. Over 7,000 German prisoners were taken that morning alone; estimates of the dead vary widely between sources, but it is certain that, for the first time on the Western Front, the defender's losses across the whole battle exceeded the attacker's.

In the game, this translates exactly into section 19.7: an enormous advantage concentrated into a single turn. From Turn 2 onward, the battle goes back to resembling the others in this manual — ordinary combat, with no shortcuts.

19.16 Wytschaete and the Irish Infantry

Historical context: in the central sector, the 16th (Irish) Division (largely nationalist and Catholic) and the 36th (Ulster) Division (largely unionist and Protestant) advanced side by side toward Wytschaete, cooperating effectively in one of the most often-remembered episodes of the battle. Less than a year later, political tensions in Ireland would erupt into the crisis that led to the War of Independence: at Messines, for a few hours, that divide counted for nothing.

Sector M3 Wytschaete represents this central objective, the most contested point of the ridge alongside Messines itself.

19.17 The Oosttaverne Line and the Limit of the Advance

Historical context: the Oosttaverne Line, the last planned objective, was not reached until 14 June, after days of German counterattacks that British artillery, by then moved forward, repelled with relative ease. Plumer deliberately chose not to push further, against pressure from those who wanted to exploit the success at once: a salient too deep, poorly connected, and out of range of its own artillery would have been a target, not an advantage.

Sector M8 represents this final objective. Section 19.9 turns Plumer's caution into a rule: full victory in the scenario rewards whoever reaches exactly this limit, not whoever goes beyond it.
