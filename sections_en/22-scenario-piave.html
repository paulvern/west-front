21.0 Scenario: Italian Front — Piave 1918

The exact reverse of Messines: here it is the defender who holds the advantage, and the attacker who must swim against the current of History.

21.1 Introduction to the Scenario

This scenario represents the Second Battle of the Piave River, also known as the Battle of the Solstice (15-23 June 1918): the last great Austro-Hungarian offensive of the war, and the defeat that marked the beginning of the end. The Austro-Hungarian player must cross the Piave, hold on to the western bank, and push toward Treviso before the river itself, in flood, turns against him. The Italian player, reinforced by British and French units, must contain the bridgeheads, absorb the initial shock, and then drive the invader back across the river.

If Messines was the scenario where the attacker starts with an almost unfair advantage, the Piave is its exact opposite: here it is the defender who starts ahead, and the Austro-Hungarian must build for himself, turn by turn, a victory that History has already denied him. Victory conditions are gathered separately, in chapter 22.0.

Historical note: this was the last offensive the Austro-Hungarian army was able to launch. Its defeat did not destroy the imperial army in a single blow, but it laid bare, beyond appeal, its exhaustion — of men, of supplies, of confidence in victory. From here to the reckoning at Vittorio Veneto in the autumn, little more than four months would pass.

21.2 Duration

The scenario lasts 7 turns, corresponding to the most intense period of the real battle, from the crossing on 15 June to the completed Austro-Hungarian withdrawal on 23 June. It does not cover the developments of early July, when the Italian Third Army recaptured the mouth of the Piave that had remained in enemy hands since Caporetto: that episode, more an epilogue than the heart of the battle, lies outside the scenario's scope.

21.6 Special Terrain of the Lower Piave

21.6.1 The Piave and River Crossings

As on the Isonzo, the river is an operational obstacle that must be prepared for, not just crossed. The rules are the same as in section 17.6.1, applied here in reverse: it is the Austro-Hungarian who must pay to set foot on the western bank.

Action Cost Feasible if... Procedure Effect
Prepared crossing of the Piave 2 LP The starting sector is controlled; no flood in progress (section 21.7) or prohibitive weather; order written. Pay 2 LP, state starting sector and target sector. A division may attack or enter a line across the river. It suffers +1 Fatigue after the action.
Crossing to Grave di Papadopoli 1 LP As above, but only toward P5. Pay 1 LP: the island is a shorter intermediate step. As above. The subsequent jump from P5 to the far bank remains at full cost.
Improvised crossing 0 LP Only if ordered and the player accepts the risk. The division attacks with VT +1 and gains +1 Fatigue before combat. Allows a risky attack without logistical preparation.

21.7 The Flooding of the Piave

This is the special rule that defines the scenario, the exact reverse of the mines at Messines: not a sudden advantage for the attacker, but a reversal of fortune at mid-scenario, in the defender's favor.

Procedure, from Turn 4 onward:
  1. From the start of the Logistics Phase of Turn 4 and for the rest of the scenario, the Piave is considered in flood.
  2. A prepared crossing of the Piave costs 4 LP instead of 2 (a crossing to Grave di Papadopoli costs 2 LP instead of 1).
  3. Improvised crossings are no longer available: the water is too high for makeshift attempts.
  4. Every Austro-Hungarian division already present in a P1-P7 sector is considered isolated for the purposes of section 14.4, regardless of a traceable path to the rear, until it retreats to the eastern bank.
  5. Italian artillery and aviation missions declared against a P1-P7 sector with at least one Austro-Hungarian division present receive +1 success, representing concentrated fire on the field bridges.
Why it works this way: between 17 and 19 June the river's level rose rapidly, sweeping away much of the Austro-Hungarian field bridging. The bridgeheads found themselves cut off from supplies and reinforcements just as Italian artillery and aviation concentrated their fire on whatever was left standing. This is not an event the Austro-Hungarian can avoid by delaying the attack: the river rises regardless, sooner or later, and the sooner it is exploited the better.

21.8 Allied Reinforcements

After Caporetto, British and French units were sent to the Italian front in support of the Fourth and Sixth Armies. In the scenario, two Allied divisions appear already deployed among the Italian forces (section 21.9.1) and count as Italian divisions for all rules purposes — capacity, initiative, victory conditions — unless stated otherwise.

21.12 The Context of the Battle of the Solstice

After Caporetto, the Italian army had reconstituted itself along the Piave line under General Armando Diaz, who had succeeded Cadorna. With Russia's exit from the war, Germany pressed its Austro-Hungarian ally to seize the opportunity with a new offensive: Venice, Padua, and Verona lay just a few dozen kilometres from the front, and a success there could have been decisive. The Austro-Hungarian plan called for a pincer: Conrad von Hötzendorf would attack from the Asiago Plateau toward Verona, while Svetozar Boroević — the same commander who had defended the Isonzo in 1916 — would cross the Piave toward Padua.

Conrad's attack stalled almost immediately, checked by the Italian Fourth and Sixth Armies, reinforced by British and French units. Boroević's, by contrast, succeeded in its first hours: roughly 100,000 men crossed the river, establishing solid bridgeheads, particularly on the Montello. But the initial success soon turned into a trap.

Operational reading: the Piave works, in the game as in reality, in two opposite phases. In the early turns the initiative belongs entirely to the Austro-Hungarian: cross, consolidate, push in depth. From the moment the river rises, the initiative passes to the Italian almost automatically — not because the Italian player does anything exceptional, but because the geography itself has taken his side.

21.13 The Montello and the Bridgeheads

Historical context: on the Montello, the wooded height north of the river's course, the corps of General Goiginger achieved the greatest Austro-Hungarian success of the battle, capturing around 12,000 Italian prisoners and 84 guns. It was precisely for this reason that, when Emperor Karl I ordered the withdrawal to the eastern bank on the evening of 20 June, Goiginger at first refused to obey: how does a corps that had just won its battle retreat? In the end, he submitted to the order.

Sector P1 Montello represents this local success — and its fragility in the face of a river that had, in the meantime, cut off every line of supply.

21.14 The Flood and the Italian Air Force

Historical context: between 17 and 19 June the Piave's water level rose rapidly, hindering or preventing the rebuilding of Austro-Hungarian field bridges. Italian aviation and artillery concentrated their fire on exactly those bridges, worsening the isolation of bridgeheads already in difficulty. Boroević completed his withdrawal to the eastern bank on 22 June, with total losses that sources estimate at between 118,000 and 150,000 dead, wounded, missing, and captured — against roughly 87,000 Italian losses.

In the game, this double blow — the river's flooding plus the concentration of Italian fire — is section 21.7: not an isolated event, but a condition that lasts for the rest of the scenario, exactly as it did in reality.

21.15 Treviso and the Limit Never Reached

Historical context: neither Boroević nor Conrad ever truly came close to Treviso, Padua, or Verona: the entire offensive never got beyond the belt immediately along the river. It was precisely this containment — not a lightning counterattack, but stubborn resistance followed by a systematic exploitation of enemy mistakes — that convinced the Allies that the Italian army, after Caporetto, was once again a reliable fighting force.

Sector P8 Treviso represents this limit that was never reached. In the scenario it can only be threatened through the Montello (P1): if the Austro-Hungarian genuinely gets there, he has already achieved an operational success well beyond the historical one.
