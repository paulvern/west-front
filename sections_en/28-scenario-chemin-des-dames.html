27.0 Scenario: Western Front — Chemin des Dames 1917

The only scenario in this manual where the real question is not how much ground is captured, but whether the attacking army remains an army still capable of attacking.

27.1 Introduction to the Scenario

This scenario represents the opening phase of the Nivelle Offensive on the Chemin des Dames (16-25 April 1917): the French attempt at a decisive breakthrough that, promised to reach Laon within a day, instead stalled after a few hundred metres, at an enormous cost. The French player must capture the limestone ridge that dominates the Aisne valley, knowing the plan is already in enemy hands. The German player must defend a line already prepared to receive the attack, on ground riddled with caves and quarries that no bombardment can fully level.

Unlike every other scenario in this manual, territorial victory here is not the only thing that matters, and may not even be the most important. The victory conditions in chapter 28.0 also measure whether the French player manages to hold his own army together — because in reality, by the end of May, he did not.

A note on this scenario's tone: the failure of the Nivelle Offensive led, in the following weeks, to a wave of collective refusals to return to the attack across much of the French army — a grave historical fact, which this manual treats with the seriousness it deserves. You will not find here any rule that turns courts-martial or death sentences into a game mechanic: section 27.8 represents only the most direct and best-documented consequence of that crisis — the collective choice to keep defending but no longer to attack — through the Morale system already present in the base rulebook.

27.2 Duration

The scenario lasts 8 turns, corresponding to the main phase of the offensive, from 16 to 25 April 1917. It does not cover the longer "Battle of the Observatories" that dragged on through the summer, nor Pétain's methodical recapture of the ridge at La Malmaison in October — an outcome that can only be inferred, not represented, given the limits of this map.

27.6 The Plan Already Known

Rule: the German had the entire French attack plan, captured in full two weeks before the offensive. Because of this, in this scenario:
  • Every German MG in sectors G1-G8 is considered fully ready from the start: no surprise rule ever applies in the French's favor.
  • In Turns 1-2, the German receives +1 to Operational Initiative.

27.7 "To Laon in a Day"

Nivelle had promised the decisive breakthrough within the first day of fighting, or the immediate halt of the offensive. The first promise was not kept; neither was the second.

Procedure, checked at the end of Turn 2:
  1. If the French does not operationally control at least 2 sectors among G1-G7 by the end of Turn 2, every French division on the map immediately takes a morale test at -2 to the roll.
  2. This test is taken only once, at the end of Turn 2, regardless of how many further turns are played.

27.8 "We Will Not Attack Anymore"

This rule represents, through the Morale system already in place, the best-documented consequence of the crisis that really did sweep through the French army starting in late April 1917: not a general refusal to fight, but a targeted refusal to go back on the attack under conditions judged suicidal, while still remaining willing to defend their own positions if attacked.

Procedure, checked at every Orders Phase from Turn 3 onward:
  1. Count how many French divisions are at Morale 2 or lower at the start of the Orders Phase.
  2. If the total is 3 or more, no French division may receive an Attack/assault order this turn, regardless of that individual division's own Morale.
  3. French divisions may still defend, retreat, or carry out any other non-offensive action normally.
  4. The condition is checked again every following turn: if overall Morale recovers and the count drops below 3, attack orders become available again from the next turn.
An important detail: the German player receives no information about this condition, and no direct mechanical benefit when it triggers. It is not a tool the German can use or exploit in play: it is simply a limit on French options, exactly as in reality German command did not, in April and May 1917, grasp the real scale of what was happening in the French lines.

27.9 The Creutes: Caves and Quarries as Shelter

The limestone plateau of the Chemin des Dames is riddled with natural caves and underground quarries — the "creutes" — used by the Germans as shelters nearly immune to bombardment.

Rule: in sectors G1-G8, artillery must obtain 2 successes instead of 1 to reduce a line's fortification by one level.

27.10 The Schneider Tanks

The combat debut of French tanks, on 16 April 1917, precedes Cambrai by seven months — but here the tanks, like the rest of the plan, were expected.

Rule: the tank battalion rules of chapter 23.7 apply (direct breakthrough, mechanical unreliability, vulnerability to anti-tank fire), with one difference: German anti-tank fire (23.7.3) succeeds on 8+ instead of 9+, because the German already knew where and when to expect the tanks.

27.14 The Context of the Nivelle Offensive

Robert Nivelle had succeeded Joffre as French commander-in-chief after earning fame at Verdun, particularly for recapturing Fort Douaumont using a creeping barrage that seemed to have found the method for breaking the trench deadlock. Nivelle promised the French government a decisive breakthrough within 24-48 hours, guaranteeing he would call off the offensive if the promise were not met. General Micheler, commanding the army group involved, voiced reservations; Nivelle threatened to resign if the offensive were denied, and the government gave way.

The plan involved 1.2 million men, nearly 5,000 artillery pieces, and 200 tanks along a front of roughly 40 kilometres, including Senegalese colonial units and a Russian contingent. But two weeks before the attack, the Germans captured the entire French operational plan, and reinforced precisely this sector in the meantime.

Operational reading: the Chemin des Dames works, in the game as in reality, as a demonstration of what happens when a rigid plan meets a prepared enemy: every turn that passes without the promised breakthrough is not just a missed opportunity, but a direct blow to the morale of an army that had been told the war would be over in a day.

27.15 Hurtebise and the Caverne du Dragon

Historical context: Hurtebise Farm and the Caverne du Dragon — an ancient stone quarry turned by the Germans into an underground fortress able to shelter hundreds of men from any bombardment — were among the points where the French advance bogged down hardest from the very first day. The Caverne du Dragon would not be captured by the French until 25 June, over two months after the offensive began, as part of the longer "Battle of the Observatories" that dragged on through the summer.

Sectors G2 Hurtebise and G3 Caverne du Dragon represent these two obstacles, the hardest on the entire map to reduce with artillery alone.

27.16 Craonne and Its Song

Historical context: the village of Craonne was almost completely levelled by the fighting. Associated with the plateau of Californie above it is the "Chanson de Craonne," a protest song against the war that mutinying soldiers spread clandestinely in the very weeks of the 1917 mutinies — singing it was, at the time, a military offense. The lyrics are not reproduced here for copyright reasons, but the song remains one of the most direct records of how the soldiers themselves experienced this battle.

Sector G6 Craonne represents this place, as symbolic as it was militarily contested.

27.17 The Mutinies

Historical context: between late April and June 1917, a growing number of French units — up to around 68 divisions, by some historical estimates, involved in some form of collective protest — refused to return to the attack, while still almost always continuing to defend their own positions if attacked. This was not a general refusal to fight, but a targeted protest against orders judged suicidal after weeks of unmet promises. On 5 May a first division openly refused to attack; on 9 May Nivelle suspended the offensive, by then too late; on 15 May he was replaced by General Philippe Pétain.
Pétain's response: Pétain responded with a combination of disciplinary measures — including courts-martial and a limited but real number of death sentences carried out — and substantial reforms: more regular rest rotations, less arbitrary leave, better food. Within a few weeks the French army recovered its ability to fight, though it gave up any new major offensives for the rest of 1917, waiting instead for greater numbers of tanks and the arrival of American troops.

Section 27.8 translates, within the limits of what a game system can reasonably represent, only the mechanical heart of this crisis: the collective choice to keep defending but no longer to attack.

27.18 Laon, the Promise Never Kept

Historical context: Nivelle had promised Laon within a single day of fighting. French troops never saw the city: not even Pétain's methodical offensive at La Malmaison, in October, reached it. The Germans would not withdraw from that sector until the spring of 1918, under the pressure of far larger events.

Sector G8 Laon represents this promise never kept. Reaching it, in the scenario, means achieving in a few days a result that never came in reality, not even in a full year.
