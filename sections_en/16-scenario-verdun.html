Part II — Scenarios

Two operational fronts to put the base rulebook to the test: Verdun 1916 and Isonzo 1916.

15.0 Scenario: Western Front — Verdun 1916

The Gateway to Verdun: an introductory scenario of pressure, attrition, and forts.

15.1 Introduction to the Scenario

This scenario represents the opening phase of the German offensive against Verdun in 1916. The German must open a breach, capture symbolic positions, and wear down the French. The French must slow the offensive's pace, preserve defensive depth, and rotate exhausted divisions.

This is a deliberately asymmetric scenario, and it is exactly this asymmetry that makes it a good test bed for the base rulebook: the German starts with the initiative, the artillery, and the preparation LP, but has a limited time horizon within which to achieve measurable results; the French, weaker in the short term, plays on the depth of his lines, long-term logistics, and the ability to bring up reinforcements before the front gives way. Every section of this chapter — sectors, forces, logistics, special rules, chronology — translates this contest, between an attacker racing against time and a defender who must hold out long enough to wear him down, into concrete terms. Victory conditions are gathered separately, in chapter 16.0.

Historical note: the scenario compresses and abstracts events that in the real battle unfolded in different phases. Some sectors, such as Vaux, Fleury, Souville, Mort-Homme, and Côte 304, are included to give operational depth even though their most intense role belongs to later phases of the battle.

15.2 Duration

The scenario lasts 10 turns. Since each turn represents one day according to the scale stated on the cover, ten turns amount to a little over a week and a half of operations: enough to build up credible offensive pressure, not enough for the German to afford turns wasted on excessive preparation. This limited duration is part of why the offensive's pace, covered in section 15.18, is one of the scenario's most important special rules.

15.5 Initial Forces

The initial deployment is not arbitrary: it reflects the real situation of the offensive's first days, with the German concentrated and ready to attack from A1 and B1/B2, and the French spread over a wider line and, in some places, still lightly fortified. Looking at the Experience and VT values in the tables below, it is immediately clear that the German fields, on average, more experienced divisions in the front line (Veteran and Regular), while the French relies in several places on Recruits: a qualitative advantage the German must exploit quickly, before French reserves and rotation, described in section 15.16, rebalance the situation.

15.10 Support Assets

Beyond the divisions, the true imbalance of resources between the two armies shows up in their support assets: the German has a markedly larger component of heavy artillery and mortars, consistent with the historical choice to open the offensive with an unprecedented concentration of fire, while the French compensates with almost twice as many machine-gun companies, reflecting a denser defense of short-range automatic weapons. These numbers are not decorative: they directly determine how many dice each player will be able to roll in the artillery missions of chapter 9.0 and in the supporting fire of chapter 12.3.

15.11 Scenario Logistics

The logistical gap between the two sides is, together with the quality of the divisions, the main driver of the scenario's initial imbalance: the German starts with more LP, receives more each turn, and can stockpile more, consistent with the role of an attacker who must be able to concentrate bombardments, gas, and air missions without running short of resources at decisive moments. The French, poorer in LP, must manage every expense more carefully: he will likely spend more LP on replacements and defensive movement than on offensive missions, consistent with the goal of holding out rather than retaking ground.

Player Starting LP LP per turn Maximum stockpile
German 18 14 25
French 12 12 22
  • In Turn 1 the German receives +6 special LP for preparation, bombardment, observation, counter-battery fire, or an offensive plan.
  • From Turn 4 the French receives +2 LP if he controls G1 or G2.

The French bonus tied to G1 Belleville and G2 Verdun from Turn 4 is not arbitrary: it represents the French logistics organization around Verdun reaching full capacity, including the famous supply road that would later go down in history as the "Voie Sacrée." In the game system, this simply translates into more LP available, but the French player should plan the early turns knowing that this extra margin will not arrive right away.

15.12 Verdun Special Rules

The rules in this section do not replace the base rulebook: they amend it at specific points to adapt it to the specific situation of February-March 1916. Every exception listed here has a limited duration — almost always stated as "in Turns X-Y" or "from Turn Z" — because it represents a temporary advantage or disadvantage tied to the opening of the offensive, not a permanent feature of the two armies. It is good practice to keep an eye on these deadlines turn by turn, because several of the scenario's balances shift precisely at the points where these special rules stop applying.

15.13 Initiative

  • In Turn 1 the German automatically has Operational Initiative.
  • From Turn 2 it is rolled normally.

15.14 Aviation

  • In Turns 1-3 the German receives +1 to air missions for initial superiority.
  • From Turn 4 this bonus no longer applies.
  • Air Interdiction is optional in the introductory scenario.

15.15 Gas

  • The German may use gas from Turn 2, maximum 1 mission per turn.
  • The French may use gas from Turn 4, maximum 1 mission per turn.
  • With an adverse wind, the gas may be cancelled or risks hitting a friendly line.

15.16 French Rotation

From Turn 4, a French division in Line 2 or Line 3 may retreat toward G1 or G2 paying only 1 LP, even crossing several friendly sectors. It cannot attack the same turn.

15.17 Forts of Verdun

  • D2 Douaumont, D3 Vaux, and F2 Souville are Fort or Fort/Height sectors.
  • Operational control: at least 2 of 3 lines controlled and no contested line.
  • Full control: no enemy division remains in the sector.
  • If a division surrenders in a fort, the enemy gains +2 additional VP.
  • A division in a fort receives +1 to the surrender test if the fortification is still level 3 or 4.

15.18 Historical Pace of the Offensive

  • +3 German VP if C2 Beaumont is controlled by the end of Turn 5.
  • +5 German VP if D2 Douaumont is operationally controlled by the end of Turn 9.
  • +3 French VP if C2 Beaumont is not controlled by the German by the end of Turn 5.
  • +5 French VP if D2 Douaumont is not operationally controlled by the German by the end of Turn 9.
Historical accuracy note: the following sections condense into 10 turns events, operational pressures, and axes of advance that in the real battle unfolded over different phases. Some sectors, such as Vaux, Fleury, Souville, Mort-Homme, and Côte 304, had their main moment in weeks or months following the very first days of the February 1916 offensive. In the game they are included to offer a broader operational map and a complete decision-making arc. These historical notes are not rules, except where explicitly stated.

15.19 The German Plan and the Logic of the Offensive

The German offensive against Verdun began on 21 February 1916 with a bombardment of exceptional intensity. The goal was not just to advance on the ground, but to force the French army to defend a sector of great symbolic value, consuming men, artillery, supplies, and morale.

Within the scenario, the German player should not think of the battle as a simple race toward Verdun. He must build a sequence of local pressures: first break the forward belt, then occupy the villages and woods, then push toward the forts and heights. Every local success must set up the next.

Operational reading: the German offensive works if it combines bombardment, aerial observation, concentration of divisions, and pace. An isolated attack can take a line, but without fresh reserves and enough LP it risks turning into sterile attrition.

15.20 Summary Chronology of the First Days

This table is not a rule in the strict sense: it is a key that links the real events of the battle to the mechanics already defined in the previous sections. It is meant above all for those approaching the scenario for the first time, to understand why certain bonuses, reserves, and objectives come into play exactly in the turns they do, instead of being available from the start or for the whole game.

Historical date Summary historical event Reflected in the scenario as
21 February 1916 Start of the German offensive with a massive bombardment of French positions north and northeast of Verdun. Turn 1: German LP bonus for preparation, bombardment, observation, and counter-battery fire.
21-22 February Extremely hard fighting in the woods and forward positions, particularly in the Bois des Caures area. A1 and the forward sectors represent the initial pressure and the resistance of the front lines.
22-23 February Progressive fall or abandonment of forward positions and villages under combined artillery and infantry pressure. Sectors C1, C2, and C3 are the first French belt to be broken.
24 February German pressure increases toward the line of forts and heights. French reserves become decisive. The first reserves enter; the French must choose whether to hold forward or rotate worn-down divisions.
25 February Fort Douaumont is captured by the Germans under surprising circumstances and with limited resistance relative to its symbolic value. D2 Douaumont is a key objective. Operational control of the fort weighs heavily in German VP.
Late February and beyond The battle widens and drags on. Vaux, Fleury, Souville, Mort-Homme, and Côte 304 progressively become central points of the fight. The scenario includes these sectors as operational depth, even though some were mainly prominent in later phases.

15.21 Local Offensives and the Scenario's Sectors

Each of the following subsections presents a sector, or a small group of sectors, from a historical point of view and then translates their characteristics into game terms: terrain, modifiers, typical fortification, symbolic value. These are not additional rules beyond section 15.3, but a commentary that helps explain why a sector has the characteristics it has, so they are easier to remember during play instead of being treated as abstract numbers on a table.

15.21.1 A1 — Bois des Caures

Historical context: the Bois des Caures is associated with the initial French resistance during the first days of the offensive. French troops, subjected to intense bombardment and then infantry assaults, put up a tenacious defense that helped slow the German pace.

From an operational point of view, a wood under bombardment is ambiguous terrain. It offers cover, but makes command, observation, orderly movement, and unit coordination difficult.

In the game: if the German attacks a wooded sector without reconnaissance, he suffers VT +1.

15.21.2 B1 Haumont and B2 Ornes — Gateway Villages

Historical context: the villages north and northeast of Verdun came under heavy pressure in the first days. Many settlements were devastated by artillery and turned into tactical landmarks.

In the scenario, Haumont and Ornes are the operational bases from which German pressure departs. They are not final objectives, but platforms for concentrating divisions, bringing in reserves, and sustaining attacks.

15.21.3 C1 Brabant, C2 Beaumont, C3 Maucourt — The First French Belt

Historical-operational context: the first French belt represents the system of forward positions meant to absorb the initial shock. Under intense bombardment, these positions could be suppressed, isolated, or made hard to reinforce.

C2 Beaumont is the scenario's central point. If the German takes it early, it opens a route toward Douaumont. If the French holds it too long, the German pace breaks down.

Scenario reading: C2 Beaumont is worth more than C1 and C3 because it is the main operational corridor toward D2 Douaumont.

15.21.4 D1 Côte du Poivre — Height and Observation

Historical context: the heights around Verdun were essential because they allowed observation, control of movement, and better direction of fire. In a war dominated by artillery, seeing meant hitting more accurately.

D1 Côte du Poivre represents a position that can influence operations around Douaumont and the central French line.

15.21.5 D2 Douaumont — The Symbolic Fort

Historical context: Fort Douaumont was one of the most important works in Verdun's defensive system. Its capture by the Germans on 25 February 1916 had an enormous moral impact. The fort's fall was surprising in both speed and circumstances.

In the game, Douaumont is the heart of the scenario. Capturing it does not automatically mean strategic victory, but it represents a major operational success.

State of Douaumont Meaning in the game
German operational control The German controls at least 2 of 3 lines and no line is contested.
German full control No French division remains in the sector.
Contested sector At least one line is contested, or a significant French presence remains.

15.21.6 D3 Vaux — The Fort's Resistance

Historical context: Fort Vaux became prominent in a later phase of the battle, mainly in spring-summer 1916. Its defense is remembered as one of the symbolic episodes of French resistance at Verdun.

In the scenario, Vaux represents the French ability to maintain defensive depth. If the German takes Douaumont but cannot push on to Vaux, his success remains limited.

15.21.7 E2 Fleury — Village in the Zone of Attrition

Historical context: Fleury-devant-Douaumont was one of the villages that became a symbol of the Battle of Verdun. Over the course of 1916 it changed hands many times and was devastated by the fighting.

In the game, Fleury represents the transition from capturing a fort to penetrating the French second defensive belt.

15.21.8 F2 Souville — The Threshold of French Depth

Historical context: Souville was a crucial position in French defensive depth. Threatening Souville meant putting pressure on the fortress's inner defense.

If the German captures Souville, the game should be heading toward a significant German victory, because the French defense has been penetrated beyond the level expected for a balanced scenario.

15.21.9 E1 Mort-Homme and F1 Côte 304 — The War of the Heights

Historical context: Mort-Homme and Côte 304 were prominent mainly in the fighting on the left bank of the Meuse, in a later phase of the offensive. They became synonymous with attritional combat and unceasing bombardment.

Their presence on the map is a reminder that Verdun was not a single corridor: lateral pressure and observation from the heights could influence the entire defensive system.

15.21.10 G1 Belleville and G2 Verdun — Rear Area and Moral Objective

Historical context: Verdun had military, political, and symbolic value. Its loss would have been an enormous blow to France. However, in the offensive's initial phase, the threat to Verdun ran through a chain of intermediate positions.

G2 Verdun should not fall often. If the German captures it, he achieves a decisive victory. If it remains French, it awards limited VP, to avoid it being a disproportionate passive bonus.
